:::chapter Jester: Blue

The first letter she sent was to her mother.

The second was to the Traveler, which was embarrassing in hindsight because she could have just talked to him directly, but writing felt more right somehow — like it made it official. Like it meant she was being serious, and the Traveler needed to understand that she was being serious.

She wrote: *I have been thinking about what you said about pranks being small acts of defiance against a world that takes itself too seriously, and I think you are right but also I think I have been using pranks to avoid things. Not all things. But some things. I think sometimes I make a joke so I don't have to say the real thing. I am working on this. Thank you for always laughing with me. Love, Jester.*

She rolled the letter up, tied it with a pink ribbon, and set it on the windowsill of their rented rooms in Port Damali. When she looked again an hour later it was gone, which meant he'd gotten it.

---

Fjord found her on the docks at sunset.

He sat down next to her without asking, which she liked. He always knew when to ask and when to just *be there*, and she was still learning to tell people which one she needed.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey," she said.

"What are you thinking about."

"The Traveler," she said. "And how weird it is that he's — you know. Real. Like, actually a god and not just a very committed imaginary friend."

"He was always real," Fjord said.

"I know. But there's knowing and there's *knowing*." She swung her feet over the water. "I feel like I've been talking to him my whole life and only now do I know his whole name. Does that make sense?"

"Makes all the sense." Fjord was quiet for a moment. The harbor sounds moved around them — rigging, water, the distant call of someone selling something. "I've been thinking about Avantika."

Jester looked at him. He had his careful expression on, the one he wore when he was being honest about something he wished he didn't have to be honest about.

"She was right about some things," he said.

"She was also trying to summon an apocalypse god."

"I know. She was right about some things *and* trying to summon an apocalypse god. People can be both." He looked out at the harbor. "She knew who she was. She knew what she wanted. I never—" He stopped. "I spent a long time not knowing those things. Faking them."

Jester leaned against his shoulder. He was warm and solid and she'd known for a while that she loved him, the way she loved all of them — messy and enormous and without quite being able to fit it into words.

"You know now," she said.

"Getting there."

"That's good," she said. "I'll help. I'm very helpful."

He laughed, quiet and real. "I know you will."

The sun went all the way down. They stayed until the harbor lights came up, because there was nowhere else to be, and because this was what it meant to be done with the thing — you stayed, and it was enough.

:::end-chapter

:::chapter Caduceus: Tea

Caduceus wrote home from every port.

He wrote about the weather and the food and the color of the sea in different latitudes. He wrote about his companions: *Jester is teaching me to prank, which I think I am bad at but she says I am good at it in a different way. Caleb is reading more for pleasure than for study, which I consider a success. Fjord is figuring out who he is and I am glad I get to watch.*

He did not write about Molly.

Not directly. Not yet. The grief was still settling — like sediment in still water, slowly finding its place on the bottom, becoming part of the soil. He knew this process. He'd watched it in his garden enough times to trust it. Things that seemed lost were frequently only underground.

He wrote: *I have been thinking about the Wildmother and what she means when she says things end. I used to think ending was simple but I think it is more like what happens in a forest when something falls — it becomes something else. The log becomes soil becomes something new. I think Molly is becoming something. I don't know what yet. But I'm watching.*

He sent the letter. He made tea. He thought about the fact that his family would read it and understand, because his family understood how things ended and became and ended and became again, and this — this more than anything — was what home felt like.

:::end-chapter
