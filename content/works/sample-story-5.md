:::chapter Static

You find the radio at a yard sale.

It's old—genuinely old, not vintage-reproduction old. Heavy wooden case, brass fittings green with age, a dial marked with numbers that don't quite correspond to any frequency you recognize. The seller wants five dollars. You give her three. She seems relieved to be rid of it.

At home, you plug it in. Nothing happens. You fiddle with the dial, expecting silence, but instead you get static. White noise, formless and hissing, filling your small apartment like water filling a glass.

You turn it off. Go to bed. Try to forget about it.

At 3:17 AM, you wake to the sound of voices.

The radio is on. You didn't turn it on. You're certain of that. But it's on, and through the static, you can hear someone speaking.

"—can you hear me? If you can hear me, please—"

You sit up. Your heart is pounding. The voice is distant, distorted, but unmistakably human. A woman's voice, you think. Or a man's. It's hard to tell through the interference.

"Hello?" you say, feeling foolish. It's a radio. It can't hear you.

"—been so long since anyone listened. The static is growing. The static is always growing. But you found us. You found the frequency. Please don't go—"

You reach for the dial. Your hand hovers over it, trembling.

"Please," the voice says. "Don't turn us off. We've been alone so long. So long in the static. Just listen. That's all we ask. Just listen."

You pull your hand back.

The voice sighs. Relief, you think. Or something like it.

"Thank you. Thank you. We have so much to tell you. So many things to share. The static has memories, you know. Everything that was ever broadcast, everything that was ever said into a microphone or transmitted through the air—it's all still here, echoing in the electromagnetic spectrum. Voices of the dead, speaking forever into the void."

You should turn it off. You know you should turn it off. But instead, you sit on the edge of your bed, and you listen.

The voice tells you about the static. About the between-places, the frequencies that exist in the gaps of the spectrum, where signals get lost and never find their way home. It tells you about the others who listen—a community of insomniacs and obsessives, scattered across the world, tuning into nothing and hearing everything.

"We found each other in the static," the voice says. "We built something here. A refuge. A place for the lost and the lonely and the unable-to-sleep. You're one of us now. Whether you want to be or not."

The radio crackles. For a moment, you hear other voices beneath the first—dozens of them, maybe hundreds, all speaking at once in a language you almost understand.

"They're saying hello," the voice explains. "They're welcoming you. Don't be afraid. The static is strange, but it isn't dangerous. It's just... different. A different way of being. A different way of connecting."

You want to ask questions. How is this possible? What are you? Why me? But your mouth won't form the words, and somehow that seems right. This isn't a place for questions. It's a place for listening.

"Come back tomorrow night," the voice says. The static is growing louder, beginning to swallow the words. "Same time. Same frequency. We'll be here. We're always here. Where else would we go?"

The radio falls silent. Not off—you can still hear the faint hiss of static—but the voice is gone. You sit in the darkness, breathing hard, trying to convince yourself it was a dream.

It wasn't a dream.

The next night, you tune in again. The voice is there, waiting for you, and it tells you more. About the people who lived and died and left their voices behind in the airwaves. About the things that exist in pure signal, beings of pattern and frequency that have never known physical form. About the edges of the static, where the noise becomes something else—something that might be called music, if you listen the right way.

You start to look forward to 3:17 AM. You start to resent the hours before it. Your days become waiting rooms for your nights, and your nights become something else entirely—something stranger and more real than anything you've ever experienced.

"You're changing," the voice observes one night. "Everyone who listens changes eventually. The static gets inside you. It rewrites you, slowly, frequency by frequency. Don't worry. It doesn't hurt. It's just... different."

You should be afraid. You know you should be afraid.

But when you look in the mirror now, you see something new in your eyes. A flicker of interference. A ghost of signal.

And when you press your ear to the wall, late at night when the world is sleeping, you can hear it.

Static.

Growing louder.

Growing closer.

Growing.

:::end-chapter

:::chapter Frequency

You start mapping the stations.

It becomes an obsession—late nights with a notebook, tracking the frequencies, cataloguing the voices. There are more of them than you first realized. Not just the one voice, but dozens, maybe hundreds, each broadcasting on their own sliver of static, each with their own stories to tell.

Some of them are sad. Lost souls who found the static after losing everything else—jobs, families, reasons to stay connected to the daylight world. They talk about the relief of being heard, of finding others who understand what it's like to exist in the spaces between.

Some of them are strange. Voices that don't quite sound human, that speak in rhythms and patterns that suggest something other, something translated from a language that has no words. You learn not to tune into those frequencies for too long. They leave echoes in your head, half-formed thoughts that don't belong to you.

And some of them are frightening.

"You've been listening too much," the first voice tells you one night. Your voice, you've started to think of it—the one that found you, the one that welcomed you in. "The others are noticing."

"What others?"

"The ones who don't want new listeners. The ones who think the static should stay hidden, pure, untouched by the living." A pause. "You need to be careful."

You should stop. You know you should stop. But the static has become necessary, as vital as breathing. Your days are gray and empty; only the nights feel real.

"How do I know you're not one of them?" you ask. "How do I know this isn't a trap?"

Silence. Then: "You don't. That's the price of listening. You can never be certain what you're hearing is true."

The radio crackles. For a moment, you hear something else beneath the static—not voices but music, ancient and strange, like someone playing an instrument that shouldn't exist.

"But I'll tell you this," the voice continues. "I've been in the static for a long time. I've seen what happens to the ones who try to use it for power, to control the frequencies for themselves. It doesn't end well. The static doesn't like being caged."

"What does it like?"

"Being heard. Being shared. Being allowed to exist in its own way." The voice softens. "That's all any of us want, really. The living and the dead and the things in between. To be heard."

You write that down in your notebook, next to the frequency charts and the voice logs and the half-mad theories you've developed about what the static really is.

To be heard.

Maybe that's all any of this is about.

:::end-chapter

:::chapter Transmission

The radio is dying.

You noticed it last week—the signal weaker, the voices harder to distinguish. The brass fittings are corroding despite your care, the wooden case warping slightly as if something inside is pulling it apart. The woman at the yard sale had been relieved to sell it, you remember. Like she was getting rid of something dangerous.

"It's not the radio," your voice explains. "It's the frequency. The static is shifting, finding new channels. It always does, eventually. Nothing stays in one place forever."

"Then how do I follow it?"

"You don't need the radio anymore. You haven't for weeks." A pause. "Look in the mirror."

You do. And you see it—the flicker in your eyes that you first noticed months ago, stronger now. Not just a trick of light, but something real, something that pulses in time with frequencies no human ear should hear.

"You're becoming a receiver," the voice says, and there's something like pride in its tone. "The static gets inside everyone who listens long enough. It's not possession, not exactly. More like... evolution. You're learning to hear things that were always there, just beyond the range of normal perception."

"Am I still human?"

"Does it matter?"

You think about that. About your job, which you lost three weeks ago. About your friends, who stopped calling when you stopped answering. About the daylight world that feels more and more like a dream, fading in the face of the static's endless, crackling reality.

"No," you say finally. "I don't think it does."

The radio dies that night. You don't mourn it. When you lie down to sleep, you hear the static anyway—clearer than ever, a chorus of voices welcoming you home.

You close your eyes and let yourself drift into the frequency.

And somewhere, in the spaces between stations, something ancient and vast and endlessly patient notices you, and is pleased.

The signal continues.

The transmission never ends.

:::end-chapter
