:::chapter Shore Leave, Day One

The Enterprise's recreational deck had thirty-seven distinct areas. Spock had visited nine of them, had found each one satisfactory as a concept and uncomfortable in practice, and had not returned to any. This was not antisocial behavior. This was optimization of time.

He told himself this on the way to the observation lounge, which was not a recreational area but which served a similar function — people came here to be quiet, which Spock respected.

The lounge was empty except for Nurse Chapel.

She was sitting cross-legged on one of the benches that ran along the viewports, eating something that appeared to be a Terran grain-based product and reading a paper book, which Spock noted as unusual. Paper books on starships were inefficient. They were also, apparently, something that Christine Chapel had opinions about.

"Commander," she said without looking up. "You're not supposed to be working during shore leave."

"I am observing," Spock said.

"Observing the stars is recreational, technically." She turned a page. "Unless you're analyzing them. Are you analyzing them?"

"I am considering whether to analyze them."

She looked up at that. She had an expressive face — one of the things he had noted about Nurse Chapel was that her face rarely concealed her opinions, which was either a professional disadvantage or an interpersonal gift depending on the context.

"Sit down," she said, with the tone she used when something was a suggestion that had the structural properties of a directive.

Spock sat down.

They were quiet for a moment. The stars moved, or rather the Enterprise moved and the stars were steady, which was a distinction that only mattered philosophically. Outside the viewport, a nebula they had passed two days ago was still visible — mauve and gold against the black, the kind of thing humans described as beautiful and Vulcans described in spectroscopic terms.

"I understand it's beautiful," Spock said.

Chapel lowered her book. "You don't need to qualify it."

"I am not qualifying—"

"You said *I understand it's beautiful* like you're defending yourself," she said. "Like someone accused you of not getting it." She looked at the nebula. "You don't have to defend yourself from liking things."

Spock was quiet for a moment.

"This is not a therapeutic intervention," he said.

"No," she agreed. "It's breakfast. You can join or not." She returned to her book. "The nebula is M-class compatible. You could do a spectroscopic analysis and also just look at it."

He looked at it.

After a while he went to the replicator and came back with tea, because seventeen hours was a long time and occupying them effectively seemed to require some form of beginning.

"What are you reading," he said.

She held up the cover. *The Left Hand of Darkness.* He recognized it — Ursula K. Le Guin, first edition 2269, a recovered print edition.

"I haven't read it," he said.

"You'd like it," she said. "It's about a person trying to understand a planet where everyone thinks differently than they do. It's very—" She paused. "Patient. It takes its time."

"I have seventeen hours," Spock said.

She looked at him sideways. "Do you want to borrow it after?"

He considered this for 2.3 seconds, which by Vulcan standards was barely deliberation at all.

"Yes," he said. "Thank you."

The nebula moved in the viewport, slow and certain, mauve and gold and neither beautiful nor not.
