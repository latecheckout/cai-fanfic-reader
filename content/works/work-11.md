:::chapter The Hospital Part

The hospital had a policy about witnesses in trauma cases that the Winchester brothers had apparently not been informed about, or had been informed about and chose to treat as an interesting guideline rather than a rule.

She'd been in the intake room for twenty minutes when the taller one — Sam, she'd gather later — appeared in the doorway with two cups of coffee and an expression of practiced non-threatening.

"Sorry," he said. "The nurse at the desk said five minutes."

"You've been here twenty minutes," she said.

"I was counting the ceiling tiles."

She looked at him. He was very large and had kind eyes and was holding a hospital coffee like it was the most important thing in the room.

"You can come in," she said.

He sat down in the chair by the window, not the one closer to the bed. She noticed that. He kept both hands visible. She noticed that too.

"I'm Sam," he said. "We were — we heard the ritual. We got there too late to stop it but we—" He stopped. Started again. "I wanted to say I'm sorry we didn't get there faster."

"You stopped it from finishing," she said. "That counts."

"Barely."

"Still."

He wrapped both hands around the coffee cup. "Is there someone we can call."

"No."

"Family?"

"No family," she said. "It's fine. I'm — I know how to do this part."

His expression did something careful. "The alone part."

"The all of it part." She looked at the window. The hospital was the kind of place where the light came in filtered and institutional, making everything the same temperature. "I've been taking care of myself for a while."

"You don't have to do that part alone," he said. "If you don't want to."

She looked at him. He met her eyes steadily — not challenging, just present.

"Why," she said.

"Because we got there late," he said. "And because I know what it's like when something like this happens and there's nobody in your corner. And because—" He shrugged, very slightly. "You seem like someone who's been on their own a long time."

She said nothing.

"You don't have to decide anything right now," he said. "I'll be in the waiting room. Dean will be in the parking lot because hospitals make him anxious and he's better out there anyway. We're not going anywhere tonight."

She thought about sending him away. She thought about it for a long time, while the filtered light moved across the floor and the hospital sounds came and went around them.

"The coffee," she said. "Is it bad?"

He looked at the cup. "Terrible," he said. "Genuinely the worst."

"Leave it," she said.

He put it on the table by her bed. Got up slowly, telegraphing everything. Moved toward the door without crowding.

"Sam," she said.

He stopped.

"Thank you," she said. "For the ceiling tiles."

He nodded once, and left, and she sat in the bad hospital light with the bad hospital coffee and thought about how it was still there — the coffee, the doorway where he'd stood, the fact of being alive to experience both.

It was a starting place. It would do.

:::chapter Month Two

The house Jody offered her was a rental on the far side of Sioux Falls — small, clean, three rooms, with a neighbor who kept chickens and didn't ask questions.

Castiel came to check on her once a week.

This had started without either of them deciding it — Sam had asked, the first few times, and then she'd started leaving the porch light on on Thursdays, and then one week Castiel had arrived on a Wednesday and she'd said nothing about the change in schedule, and now it was just what happened: Thursdays (or Wednesdays) (or occasionally Saturdays), a knock at the door, Castiel standing on her front step in his trench coat with the particular expression he wore when he was trying to correctly calibrate how to be around humans.

He was getting better at it.

"You've been reading," he said, when she opened the door. He was looking at the stack of library books on the porch table — she'd started going outside to read when the weather allowed, which Jody had said was a good sign.

"How do you know I wasn't leaving them there for atmosphere," she said.

"The one on top is open," he said. "Dog-eared at page ninety-four."

She stepped back to let him in.

He was careful in her space — had learned, over the months, the particular geometry of how to exist around someone who needed doors and windows visible and no sudden movements. He didn't do this in a way that felt managed; he did it in the way that people learned to do things that mattered to them.

"Tea," she said.

"Please," he said, and sat at the kitchen table, and folded his hands, and waited in the way that Castiel waited: completely still, without impatience, like waiting was simply a thing he was doing rather than a time he was enduring.

She made tea. She thought about how strange it was to have people who just came back. Who showed up the next week and the week after, not because they needed anything, not because there was anything to fix, just because they'd said they would.

"Castiel," she said.

"Yes."

"The week I had the bad one." The bad week. He would know which one. "You stayed until morning."

"Yes," he said.

"You didn't have to."

"I know," he said. "You were afraid. I wasn't needed anywhere else."

She set the tea down in front of him. He looked at it, and then at her.

"Is that—" she started.

"That's the whole reason," he said. "I wasn't needed anywhere else, and you were afraid, and I wanted to stay. There's no other explanation."

She sat down across from him.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay," he said.

Outside, the neighbor's chickens were making whatever sound chickens made in the late afternoon, purposeful and slightly indignant. The tea cooled between them. The window showed the yard and the road and the long flat sky of South Dakota going toward evening.

She thought: *this is what it is when people stay*.

She thought: *I should let them*.
