:::chapter Low Orbit

Dara found the other engineer sleeping in the cargo bay.

Not sprawled or uncomfortable — deliberately, carefully arranged, her jacket folded under her head, her tablet face-down on her chest, her feet pointed toward the starboard wall where the hull was thinnest and the engine vibration came through most clearly as a hum you could feel in your teeth.

"Rin," Dara said.

Rin opened one eye. "Don't tell me there's a system fault."

"No system fault. Captain wants you on the bridge for the rendezvous calculations."

"In how long."

"Twenty minutes."

Rin closed the eye. "Wake me in eighteen."

Dara crouched down. The cargo bay was cold — they kept it cold for the grain shipment, industrial-grade, the kind of cold that felt purposeful — and she could see her breath, and Rin's, visible in the half-light from the emergency strips along the floor.

"Why do you sleep down here," she said.

"Engine hum," Rin said, without opening her eyes. "It's the same frequency as my mother's weaving machine. She ran it at night. I grew up with it."

Dara sat down on the floor next to her. It was cold. She didn't leave.

"Where's your mother now," she said.

"Callisto. She's a primary-school teacher. She gave up the loom." Rin opened both eyes this time, looking up at the cargo bay ceiling, which was the same industrial gray as everything else on the *Persephone*. "I carry a recording. When I can't sleep in my bunk I come here and play it."

"And it works."

"Mostly." Rin turned her head to look at Dara. Her eyes were dark in the low light, calm and focused, the eyes of someone who was good at being exactly where they were. "Why are you here early."

Dara looked at the ceiling instead of at her.

"I wanted to see where you went," she said.

Rin was quiet. The engine hum came up through the floor, steady and low.

"And now you've seen," Rin said.

"And now I've seen."

Another silence. The *Persephone* moved through the outer belt with the patient efficiency of a ship that had made this run forty-seven times and had no more surprises to offer. Somewhere in the grain shipment behind them, something shifted and settled.

"I have a recording too," Dara said. "My uncle used to play guitar. Sunday mornings. I couldn't sleep either, growing up. I'd come downstairs and listen from the kitchen."

Rin sat up. Her jacket fell off her makeshift pillow and she picked it up, brushed it off, folded it again — neat, automatic, the motion of someone who'd been living in small spaces for a long time.

"We could play them both," she said. "See if they overlap."

It was not the kind of thing you said to someone you were simply working with.

"Yeah," Dara said. "We could."

They sat on the cold cargo bay floor with the grain and the engines and the long dark, and two recordings played over each other — guitar and loom, Sunday and childhood — and the *Persephone* carried them forward through the outer belt toward a rendezvous that would take eighteen minutes to calculate and significantly longer to explain.

:::end-chapter
