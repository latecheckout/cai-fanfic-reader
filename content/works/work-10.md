:::chapter Frenchie: The First Song He Remembers

The first song Frenchie learned had no words.

His gran used to hum it while she worked — while she hung laundry, while she kneaded bread, while she sat in the evening with whatever mending needed doing. It had a shape to it, a specific shape: three notes up, two notes down, one long note that resolved into a kind of settling, like a sigh that came out musical.

He hadn't thought about it in years. He'd thought about it in years at sea, actually — the humming came back to him when his hands were busy, when the sails needed mending or the rigging needed attention, in the same way his body remembered things his mind had let go.

"What's that," Pete asked him once, appearing at his elbow with the specific stealth of a man who had spent ten years not announcing himself.

"Something my gran sang."

"It's nice."

"It doesn't have words."

Pete sat down next to him on the deck. The evening was warm and the ship was quiet, which meant everyone was either eating, sleeping, or carefully not being in the same room as the captain and Stede. "Songs don't need words. Words come later."

Frenchie looked at him sideways. Pete had opinions about music that surprised you — he looked like a man of few dimensions and turned out, repeatedly, to be a man of many. Lucius had known this before any of them, which was either lucky or the whole point.

"How do you know," Frenchie said.

"Lucius writes things down," Pete said. "I sing them later. We work backwards." He shrugged. "Works for us."

Frenchie hummed the three notes up, two notes down, one long settling note. Pete listened.

"I can find words for that," Pete said.

"You don't have to."

"I know," Pete said. "I want to."

---

The song Pete found words for went like this: *water and time and the long way home, we carry our dead and we sail on, sail on, sail on.*

It became a crew song. These things happened on ships — a melody found words, words found a melody, and then one evening everyone was singing it and nobody remembered exactly when it became everyone's.

Frenchie told his gran about it, later, in the letter he sent from Nassau that he wasn't certain she'd receive.

*The song you used to hum*, he wrote, *is a ship song now. I hope that's alright. I think you'd like the crew. They're strange. They're mine.*

:::end-chapter

:::chapter The Swede: What He Left in Sweden

Nobody on the ship knew exactly what the Swede had left behind. This was fine with him. He'd left a fair amount behind and some of it wasn't worth bringing up and some of it he'd brought with him without meaning to, which was how Sweden worked.

He'd brought: the ability to identify seventeen varieties of mushroom by sight and smell, a recipe for a flatbread his mother made that you could cook on a ship's iron with minimal modifications, an abiding suspicion of lowlands, and the specific melancholy of a person who had grown up in a place where the dark lasted six months a year and learned to find comfort in it.

He'd left behind: his name (he'd given it up willingly, in exchange for nothing, on the third day out of Gothenburg at age nineteen, standing on the deck and looking at a sea that had no idea who he was and finding this liberating), four brothers, a dog called Bjørn, and the understanding that he would probably die before he was old.

He had not, so far, died before he was old.

He was now fairly old.

"Swede," said Frenchie, appearing with a plate of something that might be food. "How old are you."

"Old," the Swede said.

"Like, how old."

The Swede thought about this. The sun was going down off the port side in the particular way it did when they were west of the islands, burning orange and enormous, the way the sun in Sweden had never once managed in thirty-seven years of observation.

"I'm a different age here," he said.

Frenchie sat down next to him and ate whatever was on the plate. He did not press further. This was one of the things the Swede liked about Frenchie: he understood that some answers were the only answer available, and accepted them.

"The sun here," the Swede said.

"Yeah," Frenchie said. "It's something."

"In Sweden," the Swede said, "the sun is—" He stopped. Started again. "The sun is careful there. It comes out like it's apologizing. Here it just—"

He gestured at the burning west.

"Yeah," Frenchie said.

They sat and watched the sun do what it did in no uncertain terms, and the Swede thought about his name and his brothers and a dog called Bjørn, and thought about how distance was a kind of freedom and also a kind of grief and also a kind of home, and how you could carry all three at once if you had to.

He'd had to. He was fine.

:::end-chapter
