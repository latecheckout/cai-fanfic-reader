:::chapter After the End of the Book

The last prophecy in the Book of Agnes Nutter had been, in retrospect, straightforward.

*Ytte who receyveth thys laste worde from myne hande, know thys: thys is the laste worde. Go nowe. Lyve. Stoppe readynge and starte beynge.*

Anathema Device had decoded this at 3 AM on the morning after the Apocalypse That Wasn't, sitting in Jasmine Cottage with the book open and Newton asleep on the couch and the garden outside very quietly continuing to exist. She had read it three times. She had made tea. She had read it a fourth time.

She had thrown the book in the fireplace.

The book had not burned. Agnes had presumably anticipated this.

She'd thrown it in the recycling bin instead. It had gone. She'd checked the next morning. Newton had asked no questions, which she appreciated.

---

The girl showed up in April.

She arrived on a bicycle, alone, with a notebook under her arm and an expression of determined purpose that Anathema recognized with the specific discomfort of looking at a mirror tilted to a slightly different angle.

"You're the witch," the girl said.

"I'm Anathema Device. I live here."

"I know. Tadfield's only got one." The girl held up the notebook. "I'm learning. My mum doesn't know but she will eventually and I've decided it's better to be trained by someone who knows what they're doing." She looked at the garden. "You have yarrow."

"Yes," Anathema said.

"You can do second sight with yarrow. Agnes Nutter used it."

Anathema looked at this girl with her absolute certainty and her determined notebook and her specific quality of *I have decided this is happening* and thought, without meaning to: *oh. It's going to be like that.*

"Come inside," she said. "You want tea."

"I want to learn herbalism," the girl said.

"You can learn herbalism after tea."

The girl considered this with the gravity of someone evaluating a reasonable compromise. Then she leaned her bicycle against the garden wall and followed Anathema inside.

"I'm Pepper," she said.

"I know," said Anathema, who did not yet know but would.

:::end-chapter

:::chapter What Agnes Said About Students

The thing about prophecies was that they were always about something you couldn't see yet.

Agnes had been very clear about this in the annotations. *Prophesie is not a map. It is a window. Ytte shows ytte what is there, not where to go.* Which Anathema had always understood intellectually and was only now starting to understand in her actual body, the kind of understanding that came from looking at a ten-year-old girl attempting to dowse for ley lines in the back garden and thinking: *she's going to be very good at this*.

"It's moving," Pepper said, with intense focus on the dowsing rod that Newton had reluctantly made from two coat hangers. "Is it supposed to move like that."

"It's supposed to move," Anathema said.

"Toward or away."

"Toward."

"What am I toward."

Anathema looked at the rod. It was pulling northeast with unusual conviction, toward the old meadow. "The ley line runs under Lower Tadfield. You've found the eastern terminus."

Pepper looked at the rod. Looked at the meadow. Her expression was the one she wore when something had worked and she was deciding whether to be excited about it or to simply absorb it as data.

She absorbed it as data. Anathema liked this about her.

"Agnes Nutter knew about that ley line," Pepper said.

"Agnes Nutter knew about most things."

"Did she know about me."

The question was casual, asked while Pepper lowered the dowsing rod and wrote something in her notebook. Anathema looked at the garden and thought about the last prophecy, which she'd burned (or tried to burn), and about whether Agnes had left anything in the ones before it.

She thought: probably.

She thought: I'm not going to tell her that.

"Agnes knew a great deal about Lower Tadfield," she said carefully. "I think what she cared about was that the things she loved about it would continue. The ley lines. The garden. The particular quality of the sky when it rains here."

Pepper looked at the sky. It was threatening rain with considerable specificity.

"The sky here is different," she agreed.

"Yes," Anathema said. "It is."

They went inside before it started, and Anathema made tea, and Pepper covered four pages of notebook in a handwriting that was already, somehow, slightly reminiscent of Agnes's — angular and certain, the writing of someone who had no patience for ambiguity.

Anathema thought about what it meant to pass something on. About whether it was a burden or a gift, and how you could know the difference.

The rain came down on Jasmine Cottage, and the garden absorbed it, and two witches (one who'd been one her whole life and one who was just beginning) sat in comfortable silence over bad tea and good notes, and outside the ley line ran on, as it had run on, patient and deep and not caring about the calendars of the people living above it.

:::end-chapter
