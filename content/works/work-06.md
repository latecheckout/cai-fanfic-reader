:::chapter The Problem with the Evidence

The victim had been dead for six hours when Sherlock declared the case impossible.

"Not unsolvable," he clarified, crouched over the body with the particular intensity that John had learned meant *stand back and don't speak*. "Impossible. The evidence contradicts itself at the molecular level. Look."

John looked. He saw what he'd seen for the past twenty minutes: a man in his mid-forties, no signs of trauma, no signs of illness, lying in the center of his Kensington living room with an expression of absolute bewilderment and a small singed mark on his left palm.

"Carbon scoring," he said. "But no fire. No—"

"Accelerant, heat source, match, lighter, candle, spark, static discharge or electrical fault," Sherlock said. "None. And before you suggest spontaneous human combustion, the mark is external, too localized, and the tissue damage doesn't extend beneath the dermis." He stood. "The evidence says fire. The evidence is lying to me."

"Evidence doesn't lie," Lestrade said from the doorway. He had the expression of a man who was very tired and had been very tired for a long time. "You always say that."

"Evidence doesn't lie," Sherlock agreed. "Which means I'm misreading it, which means there's a variable I haven't identified, which is—" He stopped.

A woman appeared in the doorway beside Lestrade. She was in her late twenties, brown hair pulled back, dressed practically, and she was looking at the burn mark on the victim's palm with an expression John had learned to recognize: the expression of someone who understood something and was deciding how much to say.

"Auror Granger," Lestrade said, with the enthusiasm of someone introducing two weather fronts. "I'm told you've seen this before."

The woman stepped into the room. She looked at Sherlock with the same evaluating attention he was directing at her, and John watched the familiar thing happen — two very intelligent people in the same space, each determining the threat level.

"The mark is a failed containment ward," she said. "Whoever cast it wasn't skilled enough. The spell turned inward."

Sherlock blinked. In John's experience, Sherlock rarely blinked.

"I'm sorry," he said. "The *what*."

:::chapter Two Impossible Things Before Breakfast

Hermione Granger did not, as a rule, enjoy dealing with the Muggle side of investigations.

This was not prejudice — she'd been raised Muggle, she had Muggle parents, she had spent three years in a tent in the English wilderness with two people who treated infrastructure as a personal suggestion. It was simply that the Muggle side of investigations tended to involve a great deal of explaining and a great deal of time, and the explanation never quite landed in a satisfying way.

She had not expected the consulting detective.

"So your premise," he said — Holmes, the difficult one, the one whose file she'd read on the train and found inadequate — "is that someone used a form of energy manipulation that currently exists outside established physical models, produced a result that reads as thermal damage with no thermal source, and killed this man via a mechanism you're unwilling to explain further because of some kind of jurisdictional concern."

"I explained it," she said.

"You said *magic*."

"That's an accurate description."

"It's not a mechanism, it's a category." He was pacing. He did this, apparently — the file had mentioned it. *Subject thinks best in motion. Do not attempt to stop the pacing.* "Give me the mechanism."

Hermione looked at the very patient man in the jumper, who she'd identified as Watson and who had the energy of someone who had reached a personal peace with situations that made no sense. He gave her a small nod of encouragement.

"The victim was targeted with a Containment Hex," she said. "It's used to immobilize. In the hands of a trained caster it's non-lethal. This caster wasn't trained adequately. The hex overloaded and discharged through the only contact point — his left hand."

"Discharged as thermal energy."

"As a simplified description, yes."

Holmes stopped pacing. He had very sharp eyes, she noted — the kind that saw things and then immediately started working on what they meant.

"How many people in London can cast a Containment Hex," he said.

"That's classified," she said.

"That's a number under a thousand."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't say it wasn't." He looked at Watson. "We have a pool."

Watson closed his eyes briefly. "We don't need a pool."

"It narrows the suspect field considerably—"

"I'm standing right here," Hermione said.

Holmes looked at her. Something in his expression shifted — not warmth, exactly, but something like reassessment. The expression of someone who'd expected an obstacle and found, instead, a variable worth keeping.

"I know," he said. "You're going to help us. Not because your Ministry has jurisdiction and not because I've given you a reason to trust me, but because you're angry about what happened to this man and you want to know who did it."

Hermione said nothing.

"I'm right," he said.

"You're irritating," she said.

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

Watson made a sound behind her that was probably not a laugh. She turned around. His expression was completely neutral.

"Fine," she said. "I'll pull the Hexcaster Registry. It will take until morning."

"That's acceptable," Holmes said, already moving. "Watson, tea. We have work to do."
